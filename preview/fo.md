## Flannery O’Connor on intelligence and what it means to be human
In *The Violent Bear it Away*, her main character is a bright 14 year old boy, Tarwater. He was abducted by his great-uncle when he was a few months old. The old man raised Tarwater in an isolated clearing and raised him to believe they were a pair of prophets, like Elijah and Elisha. The old man told Tarwater his inaugural mission was to baptize Bishop, his mentally impaired cousin, who was in the care of Tarwater’s uncle Rayber, a school teacher and an atheist.

But Tarwater knows his great-uncle is crazy and not very smart. This clicked for him on a trip to the city shortly before the old man died. The old man went from lawyer to lawyer to take revenge against his nephew who wrote an article analyzing him. After a while Tarwater's directly rebukes the old man:
    "'They all tell you the same thing. It's only one law and it's nothing you can do about it. I got sense enough to get that; why ain't you? What's the matter with you?'"

When the old man dies, Tarwater, aided by a voice, acknowledges the old man's madness and rebells. Instead of honoring his wish to be buried with a cross at the head of the grave, Tarwater gets drunk and sets fire to the shack where his great-uncle died, and goes to the city to live with his uncle and cousin.

Rayber welcomes Tarwater with hopes to save him. You see, the old man also abducted  Rayber when he was seven years old. His parents rescued him after a few days, but Rayber believed the old man's story for along time. Only with discipline and reason was he able to fight off the spiritual impulse, which now only manifested as an occasional, irrational, burst of love for his mentally impaired son, Bishop.

Along the journey Tarwater encounters a host of characters: a capitalist that advises him to work hard to get ahead; a six year old preacher girl, used by her parents to raise money; and most crucially the voice I mentioned — a character he envisions wearing a panama hat. He identifies the voice as a stranger, but it quickly becomes a friend and mentor. The voice urges him reason, points out the old man’s obvious idiocies, and ultimately goads him into murdering Bishop to seal his rebellion — with the child dead, he could not be baptized, voiding the old man’s prophesy. But Tarwater's rebellion fails. He baptizes the child as he drowns him. When he finally returns to the clearing where the old man raised him, he discovers their Negro neighbor had buried the old man and set up a cross at the head of his grave before Tarwater set fire to the shack. And then, surrounded by another fire Tarwater frantically set to cleanse the site where he was drugged and molested by a man in a Panama hat, he finally receives the call:
    ”GO WARN THE CHILDREN OF GOD OF THE TERRIBLE SPEED OF MERCY.”

For O’Connor, the spiritual insanity of the old man is better than the rational, spiritually euthanized life Rayber represents. God works through despicable people and circumstances: an old man abducting a child to create a prophet; the exploited preacher girl to speaks directly to Rayber's soul. These terrible displays of mercy are alive with the flame of love, compared to the hollow existence Rayber invites Tarwater into, or the shallow aphorisms of the capitalist.

Two thoughts I’m noodling on:

### Thought 1
O'Connor famously writes with caricatures to make her point. She once said:
	"To the hard of hearing you shout, and for the almost blind you draw large and startling figures."

And she does this here. She writes of the platonic rationalism that surpasses love for a mentally impaired child because it isn't reasonable, and the platonic capitalism where the sales man celebrates when a man's wife dies because he no longer has to remember to ask about her in the process of making a sale.

The evils she identifies are real. But I think they primarily manifest through idolatrous systems, not individuals. This is obvious with the extremes of rationalism. Rationally speaking it is abundantly clear that people are NOT equal. And so, why not get rid of people that consume more than they produce, like Bishop? Or, why not try and produce genetically superior humans? The same with extreme capitalism. Why prioritize working conditions if people tolerate dangerous working environments? Or socialism — it is easier to create equality when quality of life is low. Even the church — if saving people is the most important thing, then it makes sense to silence sexually abused people to protect a gifted evangelist or the reputation of the church. It is ideologically captured institutions that goad people into the complicit creation of horrors, but rarely do we see individuals so warped that they fully embody these ideologies the way O'Connor portrays.

Even granting these evils, I think we discount the magnitude of genuine human flourishing produced by many of these institutions. Despite it's flaws, the church models care for the sick and poor, and is a foundational reason our secular culture sees that as good. Capitalist competition as spurred on growth, which is major contributing factor to massive quality of life improvements such as life expectancy going from about [32 in 1900 to 73 in 2023](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/life-expectancy), and extreme poverty going from about [43% in 1990 to 10% in 2025](https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/was-the-global-decline-of-extreme-poverty-only-due-to-china).

All this to say, I think reality is more ambiguous than even a tempered, "Systematic rationalism and capitalism are thoroughly bad," in the same way that Christians shouldn't agree that, "The church is thoroughly good."

### Thought 2
Bishop, the mentally impaired child, is an opportunity to reflect on what makes human's special. For a long time the easy answer was intelligence. It separates us from the animals. Bishop reminds us that intelligence isn't an intelligent foundation for delineating what makes humans special. The secular notion of universal human dignity originates in the Biblical imagination, where all people are created in the image of God. All, even the Bishops, are precious. This preciousness exists despite the fact that the Bible has categories for super-human, intelligent, powerful persons (e.g., angels and demons). This means that when faced with other super-human intelligences, like AI, we don't have to make "God of the gaps" style arguments for why humans are special. Human preciousness, even for the secular person, was never based in anything other than the fact we decided to believe we were chosen, even if we're all Bishops compared to other intelligences.
